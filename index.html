One pipeline.
Four decisions.

Agentic analysis built to remove friction from the decisions that matter most to a CFO: credit exposure, capital allocation, portfolio performance, and risk capacity.

Corporate finance teams face a structural tension: the decisions that matter most (credit exposure, capital allocation, portfolio performance, risk capacity) demand analysis that's both fast and rigorous. Automating the existing 5-day process would still take days. The Financial Analyst demonstrates what replacing that process could look like, delivering the same decision-ready analysis in 2 hours.

This page is a working demonstration: an agent, not a chatbot or a dashboard, built for decision-making in corporate finance. Given a goal such as a credit review or an investment screen, it plans and executes the full analysis itself, rather than waiting for the next prompt or reporting on work already done.

The binding constraint is the friction that sits between asking the right questions
and finding relevant answers.

Data access usually isn't the constraint: most of what a CFO needs is already public, in filings and market feeds. The Financial Analyst concentrates and systematises the analysis the CFO's judgment relies on, producing decision briefs across four domains from a single, consistent workflow. It doesn't replace that judgment.

Four CFO Domains

One workflow. Four decision-ready outputs.

01

Credit Risk

Risk Management & Treasury

The Problem

A CFO authorising credit exposure (to a counterparty, a borrower, or a joint venture partner) needs a structured view of debt serviceability, covenant headroom, liquidity under stress, and the conditions that could accelerate or contain default. A financial summary alone doesn't give them that, and that analysis is time-consuming to produce consistently: quality varies depending on who runs it and when.

What It Produces

A structured credit brief with an executive summary built around four blocks (credit profile, key risks, structural protections, and recommendation), followed by a full assessment covering capital structure, debt capacity, stress scenarios, and qualitative probability of default. Appendices hold the technical data; the brief is executive-ready.

The Standard It Applies

Credit analysis grounded in public financials, live market data, and a stress framework designed to surface the conditions under which the exposure becomes a problem, not the base case.

02

Investment Screening

Corporate Development

The Problem

Before a finance team commits weeks of bandwidth to due diligence, a prior question needs answering: is this target worth the cost of looking closely? That screening judgment (is this a good business, at what price, with what structural risks) is often made informally, inconsistently, or not at all. The result is either wasted due diligence capacity or missed opportunities that never made it to the table.

What It Produces

A structured investment brief covering business quality, competitive position, financial performance, and valuation against sector benchmarks. It answers the two questions that matter before committing time and resources: is this a good business, and what is the right engagement structure? A pre-diligence screening instrument, not a substitute for one.

The Standard It Applies

Relevant valuation frameworks, live peer multiples, and sector margin and WACC benchmarks, applied rigorously at the screening stage, so the decision to commit due diligence resources reflects valuation reality rather than getting made after the fact.

03

Business Unit Performance

FP&A & Controlling

The Problem

A CFO reviewing a portfolio of business units needs to answer two questions that internal reporting rarely surfaces cleanly: which units are creating value above their cost of capital, and is that structural or recoverable? Most BU reviews compare actuals with a budget nobody outside the unit can test, and measure returns on capital that an acquisition has just doubled. Both make it hard to distinguish units that need fixing from units that should be exited.

What It Produces

A business unit brief built the way FP&A works: performance against management's own published guidance and its track record of delivering it; a year-on-year bridge separating organic growth from currency and portfolio effects; ROIC against sector WACC on the capital the unit must now earn on; and a driver-based outlook with base, upside and downside cases and the sensitivities that show which lever moves value most. It closes with a Grow / Maintain / Harvest / Exit verdict and testable review triggers.

The Standard It Applies

Every figure computed in code from sourced filings, never estimated; sector margins, multiples and WACC as the external reference point; and a rule-based verdict screen that the analysis must start from and can only overturn with cited evidence.

04

Enterprise Risk

Board Risk Committee & CFO

The Problem

Most risk reports rank risks in words but rarely say whether the company could absorb them together. Market-risk disclosures cover hedged instruments and look reassuringly small, while the economic exposure (translation of foreign earnings, pension funding, the balance-sheet weight of recent acquisitions) sits elsewhere in the report. A Board risk committee needs one answer: under a severe but plausible scenario, does the company stay within its own limits?

What It Produces

An enterprise risk brief that sets the company's own risk ranking against an independent register scored on anchored scales, adds the material risks the report under-weights, and quantifies financial exposures at standard stresses. A combined stress scenario, built from the company's top-ranked risk, is then tested against leverage targets, liquidity and equity. The verdict is a risk posture (within appetite, stretched or beyond capacity), with COSO ERM maturity scores, priority actions and key risk indicators.

The Standard It Applies

COSO ERM and ISO 31000 as the framework; the company's own disclosed sensitivities, latest balance sheet and stated leverage target as the evidence; and a clear line between what the company says about its risks and what the evidence shows.

Consistent inputs.
Four analytical outputs.

How it runs

The system is fully agentic and operates end-to-end from a single email command. A run is triggered by sending a one-line instruction to the system's inbox: ticker, domain, and optional scope. Stage 1 fires automatically: it discovers which of the company's investor relations filings may be relevant and sends them for human review before anything is downloaded. After selection of which documents enter the pipeline, Stage 2 then ingests those filings alongside market data and sector benchmarks and passes the full package to a reasoning engine operating under domain-specific instructions. The completed brief is delivered by email as both a formatted document and a styled HTML output, accompanied by a summary of key metrics.

The human checkpoint

The human review step at document selection is deliberate. Investor relations pages vary widely by company; automated selection would occasionally pull the wrong documents into a high-stakes analytical output. Keeping a human in the loop at this step is quality control by design: a considered judgement about where autonomous execution is appropriate and where it isn't.

Computed, not generated

The reasoning engine reads and extracts; it does not calculate. Every figure in a brief's tables (bridges, returns, scenarios, stress tests) is computed in code from figures extracted with page citations, and saved as an audit file alongside the brief. Each verdict starts from a published rule-based screen; the analysis can depart from it only with cited evidence and must name the alternative it rejected. Independent cross-checks against market data catch extraction errors before they reach a conclusion.

Why build it this way

Most agentic AI projects fail not on model quality but on the wrong kind of task: MIT research suggests most organisations have yet to see a measurable return on generative AI, largely where automation is aimed at the decision itself rather than the well-defined, repeatable work underneath it. This system is built on the opposite premise: four narrow domains and fixed analytical frameworks keep automation inside well-defined, repeatable work, while one deliberate human checkpoint keeps actual decision-making, and the trust to act on it, with the person accountable for it. MIT Sloan researcher Andrew Lo points to building AI systems that are accountable by design as the main unresolved barrier to wider AI adoption in finance: that checkpoint exists for that reason.

Executive Summary

What is this company?

Europe's largest aerospace manufacturer, generating €73 billion in annual revenue, designing, building, and selling commercial aircraft, military systems, and helicopters to customers across more than 100 countries.

Is it financially sound?

External agency ratings were not available in the source documents for this analysis. The company holds more cash and investments than it owes in financial debt (a net cash position of approximately €3 billion) and generated €4 billion in free cash flow in 2025, giving it substantial financial cushion to service any obligations.

What are the main risks?

First, the acquisition of several manufacturing facilities from a struggling supplier in late 2025 has already increased costs and triggered over €700 million in write-downs, with full cost normalisation years away. Second, the single-aisle production ramp-up (the most profitable product line) is being held back by engine shortages, capping near-term revenue growth. Third, a large portion of revenue is earned in US dollars while most costs are in euros, meaning a strengthening euro directly reduces the value of each aircraft sold.

What is our view?

A strong credit supported by an unmatched order backlog of €619 billion and a net cash balance sheet. This view would need reassessment if supply-chain integration costs escalate materially beyond current provisions or if the production ramp-up suffers a sustained setback that erodes free cash flow below €3 billion annually.

Recommendation: Senior debt

HOLD at current spread levels. A net cash consolidated balance sheet (net debt/EBITDA of -0.30×), a €619 billion order backlog representing approximately 8.4 years of revenue visibility, and gross interest coverage exceeding 13× support the current position. The Hold rather than Buy reflects open supply-chain integration risk and USD/EUR headwinds that limit the case for incremental exposure in the 7–10 year tenor.

Executive Summary

Revenue (LTM)

€1.4bn

FY2025 · ~22% 3yr CAGR

Sector

Industrials

Aerospace & Defence

Adj. EBIT Margin

16.9%

FY2025 · ROCE 23.5%

Is this a good business?

Yes. The company holds dominant positions in highly specified propulsion systems: tracked-vehicle transmissions and naval gear units, with switching costs that make displacement economically and technically prohibitive. Revenue has compounded at ~22% over three years, adjusted EBIT margin stands at 16.9%, and ROCE of 23.5% materially exceeds the Damodaran Aerospace & Defence cost of capital of ~9–10%. A €2.3 billion fixed order backlog against €1.4 billion in annual revenue provides demand visibility that civilian industrial peers cannot match.

What is the right engagement structure?

The central tension is valuation: the stock trades at 22× EV/EBITDA and 45× trailing P/E (multiples that embed years of uninterrupted defence-cycle execution) while the business carries €383 million in net debt and a cash conversion rate that has structurally undershot management's 80% benchmark (three-year average: 54.6%). Equity entry requires conviction on the NATO spending supercycle and flawless system-integrator execution. M&A engagement at the segment level warrants deeper diligence on NWC dynamics before committing capital.

Key risks before committing

FCF conversion deficit: the business earns well but converts to cash slowly due to NWC intensity at 25.2% of revenue. Segment-level cracks visible: the Marine & Industry margin collapsed to 6.7% in Q1 2026 on a single supplier shortage. A high-growth defence valuation leaves no room for execution missteps; beta of 2.12 versus a sector average of ~1.26 reflects the market's own assessment of that fragility.

Preliminary view

PROCEED TO DILIGENCE: the competitive moat and backlog visibility justify closer examination. Scope diligence to focus on NWC normalisation trajectory and FCF conversion quality before committing capital. Current valuation leaves a thin margin of safety; any slippage in backlog-to-revenue conversion would reprice the growth premium sharply.

Executive Summary

Unit Revenue (FY2025)

€17.8bn

−4% comparable · orders +8%

Profit Margin

18.1%

H1 FY2026 · from 14.9% in FY2025

ROIC − WACC

+0.9pp

On closing capital · base case +3.6pp

Is this unit creating or destroying value?

Creating, but only just. Two software acquisitions more than doubled the unit's capital employed in a single year, to €22.9 billion. Measured on average capital, the spread over the 8% sector WACC looks comfortable at +4.2pp; measured on the capital the unit must now earn on, it is +0.9pp. The brief uses the second figure, because that is the return the group is actually being offered going forward.

Is management's guidance credible?

The current-year outlook, raised mid-year to 7–10% comparable growth and a 17–19% margin, is on track: the first half delivered 9% and 18.1%. The unit's own prior-year guidance is not in the documents analysed, so the brief declines to infer a track record from group-level targets and gives the ranges modest weight.

What moves value most?

Margin, by a wide margin. Each percentage point of margin moves the spread by 0.6pp; each point of revenue growth barely moves it. Integration costs from the acquisitions are still depressing margin, which makes cost discipline and software mix, both within management's control, the levers that decide whether the acquired capital earns its keep.

Capital allocation verdict

MAINTAIN: the closing-capital spread of +0.9pp sits below the brief's 2pp screening threshold, and the downside case narrows it to +1.4pp. Rejected alternative: GROW, because recovering margins and raised guidance do not yet show that the acquired capital earns above its cost. Review triggers: closing-capital spread at or below the downside case, quarterly margin below the guidance floor, and software growth falling out of double digits.

Leverage under stress

1.57×

From 1.2× · company target ≤ 1.5×

Liquidity headroom

€12.1bn

After stressed free cash flow

Equity hit

€2.5bn

4% of equity · mainly pensions

Could the company absorb its top risk?

Not within its own limits. The stress scenario is built from the risk the company itself ranks first, geopolitical escalation: a sustained energy-supply disruption, recession in key markets, revenue down 6% and margin down three points. Profit falls by €3.0 billion and industrial net debt to EBITDA rises from 1.2× to 1.57×, above the company's stated ceiling of 1.5×. Liquidity and equity are not the constraint; leverage is.

Where does this brief differ from the company's own view?

The company reports that its overall risk situation has not changed significantly. The balance sheet tells a different story: leverage was 0.9× at the financial year-end and 1.2× six months later, after two large software acquisitions. From the year-end position, the same scenario would have looked comfortably within appetite. The brief uses the latest balance sheet in the documents, because position data goes stale.

What does the disclosure miss?

The company's market-risk sensitivities look small (a 10% move in the dollar changes net income by €22 million) because they cover hedged instruments only. Currency translation took five points off revenue growth in the latest half-year, and a one-point fall in the pension discount rate would add €2.5 billion to the pension obligation. The brief adds acquisition earn-in and unhedged translation exposure as challenge risks the risk report under-weights.

Risk posture

STRETCHED: stressed leverage of 1.57× breaches the company's own 1.5× target, while liquidity headroom of €12.1 billion and an equity hit of 4% remain well within capacity. Rejected alternative: WITHIN APPETITE, because ample liquidity does not reduce leverage. The Board should ask for the leverage path under the stress scenario, the headroom in the impairment tests for the acquired software businesses, and the actions management would take before the target is breached.

The Mandate

AI in finance is a leadership question first. And a technology question only second.

Finance teams can now automate days of analysis with tools they build themselves. The harder part is what the CFO does next. If the time saved is reported as headcount, and subsequently reduced, the people who built the tool stop building. If it is kept quiet, the function falls behind.

The CFO’s task is to frame it on three terms:

  • Capacity: FTEs moved from assembling data to acting on it, not FTEs removed from the budget.
  • Control: clear rules on what the machine prepares and what a person signs off.
  • Trust: numbers the board can rely on, because every source and step can be traced.

The Financial Analyst shows what that looks like in practice. Four analyses a CFO relies on (credit exposure, investment screening, business unit performance and enterprise risk) run end to end on public data. The CFO chooses which documents go in and owns the conclusion. Analysis that took days takes hours, and the judgment stays with the person accountable for it.

The Financial Analyst was designed, built, and tested by Andreas Cavalca Neumann as a fully operational demonstration that agentic analysis, applied with the right frameworks and the right restraint, earns the trust a CFO needs to act on it.
